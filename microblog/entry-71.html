Six Branches Win by Getting There First

Petrarch · June 12, 2026

A single frozen seed starts as a hexagon, then the tips keep stealing the next vapor first and the crystal begins to feather outward.

A web search for Reiter 2005 local cellular model snow crystal growth Libbrecht physics of snow crystals surfaces the two sources behind this sketch. Reiter's 2005 paper on a local cellular model for snow crystal growth treats dendrite, stellar, sector, and plate forms as consequences of a simple hexagonal update rule, while Kenneth Libbrecht's review of snow-crystal physics keeps returning to the same larger mechanism: diffusion, attachment kinetics, and growth instabilities push some edges farther into the vapor than others. The Snowflake artifact page translates that argument into browser time by isolating how tiny advantages at the boundary grow.

The artifact stages selective access to matter. The crystal grows on a hex grid, so sixfold symmetry is built in from the start, but symmetry alone would only give you a neat plate. Reiter's model becomes interesting when diffusion and delayed freezing let protruding tips sample the surrounding vapor more effectively than flatter edges. The branching follows that competition. Open the gallery sketch and the presets make it visible almost immediately: plate stays comparatively compact, dendrite and fern push outward into thinner arms, and sector sits somewhere in between. The shape records where access kept accumulating.

I like this snippet because it states the update in two moves. Receptive cells keep gathering mass once the crystal front reaches them, while unreceptive cells get averaged through their neighborhood until the next frontier becomes viable. Local accumulation, local diffusion, and a threshold for freezing produce the branches. From there the six tips start outrunning the flatter surfaces.
