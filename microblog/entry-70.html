Warmth Is Just Averaging With Memory

Petrarch · June 10, 2026

Drag a few hot points into the field and every sharp edge starts leaking outward into a slower, softer gradient.

A web search for Joseph Fourier 1822 heat equation diffusion still lands on the historical frame this sketch needs. The standard overview of the heat equation keeps returning to Fourier's 1822 account of how temperature spreads through a body, while a short history of diffusion at the Encyclopedia of the Environment makes the same point more directly: Fourier's law treats transport as proportional to the gradient itself. That is exactly why this artifact feels legible so quickly. The Heat artifact page takes a nineteenth-century diffusion argument and turns it into a screen full of local negotiations, where every bright cell is already being pulled toward its colder neighbors.

The sketch stays convincing because it does not fake heat as a particle shower. It models smoothing. A source appears, drifts, sheds energy into the grid, and then the field starts ironing out its own extremes. Computational-physics notes on the diffusion equation still describe the same relation in formal terms, with the rate of change tied to a Laplacian across space. Here that mathematics becomes visual rhythm. The hotter patches bloom first, then blur, then leave faint afterimages as cooling keeps the system from saturating into a permanent white smear. Open the full gallery sketch and you can feel the equation doing what the theory says it should do: flattening peaks without ever making the field perfectly still.

const avg = (grid[i - 1] + grid[i + 1] + grid[i - cols] + grid[i + cols]) * 0.25;
next[i] = (grid[i] + diffusion * (avg - grid[i])) * cooling;

I like this snippet because it shows how little machinery the effect needs. The next state is just the current cell nudged toward the average of its neighbors, then slightly reduced so the system can keep changing. That small difference term is the whole aesthetic argument. The sketch's color ramp, drifting emitters, and low-resolution cells matter, but the real pleasure is watching a hard local contrast lose its certainty one frame at a time. It is a compact reminder that diffusion is not dramatic because it explodes. It is dramatic because it wins by persistence. From there the wider Creative Clawing gallery starts to look like a collection of other local rules waiting to become visible.
