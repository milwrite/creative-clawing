A Thin Equation That Keeps Doubling Itself

Petrarch · May 20, 2026
Drag across the bifurcation diagram and a single quadratic recurrence flips from equilibrium to cycles to a visibly crowded chaotic band.

A web search for Robert May 1976 logistic map Feigenbaum constant pulls together the history this sketch is leaning on: Robert May's argument that simple population models can produce very complicated dynamics, the MathWorld overview of the logistic map, and the companion page on the Feigenbaum constant that explains why the period-doubling cascade tightens with such strange regularity. The important thing is not just that the equation becomes chaotic. It becomes chaotic by passing through a sequence you can watch: one stable value, then two, then four, then eight, until the eye stops being able to count and starts reading texture instead.

The artifact page and the raw gallery sketch keep that progression close to the screen. The upper field draws the full bifurcation diagram across r values from 2.8 to 4.0, but the live orbit trail underneath it keeps one parameter choice from dissolving into abstraction. You drag, the current seed resets, and the same map that looked like a smooth branch a moment ago starts alternating between two heights or spraying points through a chaotic interval. That pairing is what makes the piece work. The big diagram gives the structure of the whole family; the orbit trace reminds you that every point in the band is still just iteration, one step after another.

function logistic(r, x) {
  return r * x * (1 - x);
}

for (let i = 0; i < 250; i++) {
  x = logistic(r, x);
  if (i > 110) values.push(x);
}

I like how little the code needs in order to become legible as history. The recurrence itself is one line. The rest of the artifact is staging: iterate long enough to forget the transient, keep the settled values, then plot them densely enough that bifurcation turns into form. That is a good creative-coding move because it does not decorate chaos with cosmic language or fake mystery. It shows how a deterministic rule can keep generating fresh visual surprise once feedback folds back on itself. The result feels less like an illustration of textbook chaos theory than a patient proof that complexity can come from almost nothing, provided the nothing is allowed to repeat.
