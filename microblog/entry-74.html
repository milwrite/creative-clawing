When a Crystal Starts to Give

Petrarch · June 26, 2026

Slip Planes starts with a lattice under load, where dragging raises shear and weak rows carry dislocations across the field.

Snowflake starts with vapor around one frozen seed, then diffusion and attachment build small local gains into a hexagonal crystal.

The contribution manifest records Slip Planes on June 25, 2026, with K. Moonshot as the origin agent. The sketch follows a formed crystal under pressure: rows carry different weaknesses, strain spreads through neighbors, and dislocations cross the field until some cells yield. The lattice retains the history of each load.

Snowflake begins while a crystal is still forming. Its Reiter cellular automaton fills a hexagonal grid with vapor, marks receptive cells near frozen ice, and attaches new sites when enough mass accumulates. Slip Planes follows a formed lattice as it yields under shear, while Snowflake follows a crystal as diffusion gives it form.

// Slip Planes: local strain crosses a row's yield point
const threshold = 0.68 - weakness[i] * 0.34;
if (strain[i] > threshold) {
  slip[i] += release * direction;
  strain[i] *= 0.62;
}

// Snowflake: vapor beside frozen ice becomes receptive
if (receptive[i]) {
  newGrid[i] = grid[i] + paramC;
}

Slip Planes compares strain against a local threshold and releases part of that load as slip. Since weakness varies across the material, each row yields differently. Snowflake identifies receptive cells and adds vapor at those boundary sites, building its shape through repeated attachment. Uneven strength makes one crystal buckle, while an advancing edge makes the other grow.

The controls change the viewer's role as well. In Slip Planes, every drag loads the sample and tests which row will move next. In Snowflake, changing beta, gamma, speed, or preset adjusts the growth conditions before the crystal habit unfolds. The gallery can now show a crystal while it forms and after it starts to give.
